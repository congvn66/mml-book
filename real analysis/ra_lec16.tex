\section{Subsequences}
\begin{definition}
$\{p_n\}$ is a sequence, let $n_1<n_2<n_3<...$ be an increasing set of indices. Then $\{p_{n_1}, p_{n_2},...\}$ is a subsequence of $\{p_n\}$, denoted as $\{p_{n_i}\}^{\infty}_{i=1}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{note}
Here, you have to note that, a subsequence in this context, is an infinite subsequence. A finite subsequence only appears when we take the subsequence from a finite sequence.
\end{note}

\begin{proposition}
Every subsequences of a converging sequence converge.
\end{proposition}

\begin{definition}
A compact metric space $X$ is called sequentially compact if every sequence has (at least) a converging subsequence (to a point in $X$). 
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}
If $X$ is compact, then $X$ is sequentially compact.
\end{theorem}

\begin{note}
With this theorem, the imagination may hard for you. But first, the meaning of the theorem, is just you pick any sequence (of course infinite) in a compact set, that sequence will always have (at least) a converging subsequence. The key thing that you have to imagine is how to construct a infinite converging subsequence from any infinite sequence in that compact set. Imagine a sequence $A$ in a compact set $X$. Because $X$ is compact, then we can cover $X$ by finite number of fixed radius ball (definition of compact). And again, because $A$ is infinite, 1 ball must have infinitely many point (Dirichlet's pigeon). Now we have a ball that contains infinitely many points, again, repeat the process but with smaller radius and consider only with the previous infinitely chosen points. After that we just choose point $p_{n_i}$ with $n_i \in \text{ ball}_i$.
\end{note}
